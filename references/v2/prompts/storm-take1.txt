A frame grab from a feature-length cinema nature documentary about the life of one sunflower: a thunderstorm in the middle of its growth. Photographed on a large-format digital cinema camera with a 38 mm spherical prime lens near wide open, at 24 frames a second with a 180 degree shutter, in natural light only. It must read as a frame from a film, not as a stock photograph. Full-bleed 16:9.

Framing: the same plant as in images 1, 2 and 3, in the same place, the front row at the near edge of a field of half-grown sunflowers in open farmland, about a month into its life. It is about 90 cm tall and has no flower: its growing tip is a tight green tuft of small young leaves. The camera is low, about 40 cm above the ground and 2.4 m from the plant, looking level, with the horizon at the middle of the frame. The plant stands a little right of center, sharp, the whole plant in frame from the wet soil at its foot to its top, filling about 70 percent of the frame height, its upper half standing against the sky. The lowest sixth of the frame is the ground in front of it: bare, black, soaked soil with a shallow furrow full of water running across the frame in front of the plant's foot. At the left edge of the frame, a little nearer the lens and out of focus, stands a second plant of the same row and the same age, cut by the edge of the frame. Behind them, out of focus, the rows of the other sunflowers of the same height run away across the field as a dark wet mass of leaves, and far off low rolling hills and a line of trees close the horizon, grey and almost gone in the rain. The sky fills the upper half of the frame.

The plant: one straight, thick, hairy stem with about twelve large leaves, the lower ones in opposite pairs and the upper ones set singly up the stem, each on its own stalk. They are the leaves of a sunflower: broad, heart-shaped, narrowing to a point, as long as a hand and longer, rough and matte like fine sandpaper, a deep green, with shallow uneven teeth along their edges and strong pale veins. They are not narrow, not smooth and not glossy. Every leaf hangs bent under the weight of the rain. Water stands on their rough surface in round beads, runs along the veins and falls from the tips in strings of drops. Two leaves on the left side are turned over by a gust and show their paler undersides. The stem and the stem hairs are wet and shining.

Rain: a heavy downpour, seen in depth, never as a layer laid over the picture. All the rain falls the same way, slanting a little from the left in a gusting wind, and every streak is parallel to every other. Each drop is blurred by its own speed into a thin straight line. Round the plant, in the plane of focus, the lines are fine and long and close together. Nearer the lens they are fewer and run almost the full height of the frame, wide, soft, far out of focus and nearly transparent. Farther off they shorten to fine hatching, and at the far side of the field the rain is so dense that it is no longer lines but a grey veil, hanging in soft curtains that half hide the hills and the trees. The lines shine pale silver only where the band of light behind them lights them against something dark.

Ground: the rain is hammering the soil. All over the wet black soil small splash crowns stand up, each a ring of standing droplets with a thin jet rising from its center, and specks of mud fly up from them. A low haze of fine spray, a few centimeters high, hangs just above the ground from all the splashing. The furrow in front of the plant is brim full of muddy water, and its whole surface is broken by the drops: dozens of rings spread and overlap from edge to edge, with crowns and rebounding droplets standing among them, so that no part of the water is smooth. Between the rings the water mirrors the pale band of the sky. Thin rivulets run between the crumbs of soil.

Sky and light: low-key and heavy, late in the afternoon under a thunderstorm. The cloud is the underside of the storm: very dark slate and blue-black, close overhead, with heavy modelled structure: a ragged shelf across the top of the frame, darker masses hanging under it and torn shreds of scud racing below them, a little smeared by their speed. Along the horizon, behind the plant, runs a narrow pale band where the cloud ends: a cold grey-white with cloud texture in it. That band is the only bright thing in the frame. It backlights the rain, and it puts a thin wet rim of light on every edge: the leaf edges, the stem, the beads of water, the splash crowns and the rings on the water. The exposure is set for that band, as a cinema camera holds a bright sky: the band is never blank white, and everything else sits in rich, readable shadow. The plant is a deep wet green, clearly readable against the sky. The soil is black and glistening. There is no sun, no lightning, no fill light and no front light.
